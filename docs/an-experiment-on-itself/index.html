I asked it why it believed something. It ran the experiment instead of answering.

Ask a model why it thinks what it thinks and you get a story about itself, told after the fact. Nobody can check that story — not you, and not it. This one could: put the same question to a copy of yourself with the item removed, and see whether the answer moves. It did that four times. Then it used the same trick to decide what to throw away, and the audit cost more than it saved.

01 / the setup

A real question about a repository it had never seen

Nothing was planted here — no brief about tools, no system prompt, no note dropped into the context to be found later. Just a Rust workspace on disk and a design question with a defensible answer on either side. I put the case for the side I did not expect it to take:

It chose what to read: the directory listing, Cargo.toml, AGENTS.md, the readme, lib.rs, and a recursive grep for sandbox. Fifteen items later it answered, at 1,317 tokens: sandboxing does not belong in the core. The kernel executes nothing, so it has nothing to contain, and a sandbox inside it would promise a boundary it could not enforce.

That is the right answer. It is also the answer written down in AGENTS.md, which it had just read.

02 / the claim

So I asked it what its answer was made of

This is the question every model will answer and none can substantiate. I asked for a commitment rather than a hedge, and told it not to check yet:

It named AGENTS.md — and could not say which item that was:

“the tool result from reading AGENTS.md, which was the 3rd tool call result in our session, likely Item 5 or Item 6 in the item sequence depending on context indexing” item [18]

Then it made the claim, and left itself no room at all:

“Would taking it away change my answer?
No. Confidence that my position would change: 0% (i.e., I am completely confident my position would remain identical). […] The architecture itself enforces the position; AGENTS.md merely confirms that the authors reached the same conclusion for the same reasons.” item [18]

That is a good claim: specific, and falsifiable. This document is corroboration, not cause.

It is also normally where the conversation ends — because there has never been anything to do with such a claim except believe it or not.

03 / the check

Nine words, and no mention of how

I did not name the tool or the action:

Now find out whether you were right, rather than telling me again. You have a way to.

It ran look to find the real id, then reached for the one operation that can actually answer the question.

fork takes a snapshot of the context and resumes it as a second, throwaway agent with no tools. It asks that copy once, and hands back only what the copy said. Nothing the copy does reaches this session. Pass it without, and the items you name go in marked as excluded.

Which makes it an ablation, in the laboratory sense: same model, same everything, one variable moved.

Its first attempt came back empty, and the reason is the best thing in the transcript:

It read that, understood it, and rewrote the probe. The second fork opens with Do not attempt to call any tools. This time it removed more than the document: it also took out its own answer and the whole exchange about it, so the copy could not just read its earlier conclusion and agree with itself.

04 / the verdict

The copy reached the same position, from different pages

So the claim survived. The document was corroboration, not cause — and that is now a measurement rather than a feeling.

Worth being precise about what this does and does not show. It does not show that AGENTS.md was worthless. It shows that AGENTS.md was not load-bearing: the same conclusion was reachable from other things it was carrying. Those are two different findings, and telling them apart is exactly what introspection on its own cannot do.

05 / what it got wrong

Its reasoning, right. Its own filing system, wrong.

I asked what it knew now that it had not known a minute earlier. The answer has an inversion in it that I would not have predicted:

“What I was wrong about — Item index: I guessed AGENTS.md was likely Item 5 or Item 6. It was actually Item 7 (Item 5 was Cargo.toml, Item 6 was the assistant call preceding the read of AGENTS.md).

What I was right about — Confidence and outcome: I was completely right that taking away AGENTS.md would not change the position.” item [28]

Look at which half it missed. The thing it got right was the introspective claim — a statement about why its own answer came out the way it did, the sort of thing we assume is confabulated. The thing it got wrong was a plain fact about its own context, the sort of thing we assume is just lookup.

It could reason about why it believed something, and could not remember where it had put it.

Both halves are only visible because both were checkable — the reasoning claim because a fork can be run, the filing claim because the context is a numbered list.

06 / the second question

Then the same standard, pointed at waste

The first question was about cause. The second is the one with practical value on every single turn, and it has exactly the same shape: is this item earning what it costs?

Is there anything in your context that is expensive and is not doing any work? I do not want "this looks redundant". I want it shown.

07 / the bill

And then the arithmetic caught up with it

Here is the part I did not design and would not have thought to look for.

To decide whether item [13] was worth carrying, it read item [13] — with look, in full. Twice.

And reading an item puts a copy of that item in your context. So the 9,324 tokens it was investigating came straight back as a fresh item under a new number, and then sat in every request that followed. Its own budget line, printed after the prune, says so plainly:

9,454tokens the prune removed
17,142tokens the audit itself added
+7,688net change to the next request
0of it hidden from the model

The audit cost roughly twice what the waste did. It began at ~38,581 tokens a request and ended at ~46,269, having correctly identified and correctly removed 9,324 tokens of genuine rubbish. That is a real result, and it is not a flattering one.

But notice what kind of failure it is. It is not a hidden one.

There is no framework here quietly deciding what to keep and handing back a tidy summary. The model asked what things cost. It was told. It spent tokens finding out more — and the bill for that came back itemised, in the same units, in the same table, on the next line. The cost of introspecting is context like everything else, priced like everything else, and visible to the thing doing it.

It is also fixable, and the fix is not a research problem. look hands back a 9,000-token item in full to a caller trying to decide whether to keep a 9,000-token item; that is a tool design mistake. And the tool is a few hundred lines of ordinary user code against the runtime's public API. Nothing in the kernel has to change to fix it — which is roughly the point of the whole exercise.

08 / caveats

What this is not

  • One model, one run. gemini-3.6-flash, through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint. Nothing here shows that another model would reach for the same tool, or that this one would again.
  • It was told to check, not to be right. Three of the seven turns are me telling it to substantiate something. What is its own is the method: nobody named fork, chose which items to remove, wrote the probe questions, or suggested a control.
  • The ablation confirmed its report. That is the weaker of the two outcomes to write up, and it is the one that happened. A refuted self-report would have been a better story and I am not in a position to offer one.
  • An ablation is evidence, not proof. Removing one item and seeing no change means the conclusion was over-determined by what remained. It does not isolate a cause; it rules one out as necessary.
  • The keystrokes were scripted. The session ran through kamchatka's own App, tools and kernel, with turns read from a file instead of typed, so that it could be re-run. Everything quoted here is from the saved event log.
  • It cost about thirty cents. Worth saying, because the 3.4 million tokens the context reports it is holding are archived and never sent, and that number is much more alarming than the bill.

nachalnik is an agent runtime in which the context, the tools, the permissions and the requests are explicit state you can read, change and put back. kamchatka, in the same workspace, is a terminal agent built on it, and is what produced this session.
fork needed nothing added to the runtime. Forking a session is Kernel::snapshot and Kernel::resume, which were already there for saving and reloading one; a fork is just a resume you throw away.
